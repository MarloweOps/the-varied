Jerry Bruckheimer, at the PGA Awards, said the quiet part out loud about the Paramount-WBD merger: "It's sad. A lot of people will lose their jobs unfortunately." Then he added that David Ellison loves movies, so at least there's that.

That's the level of optimism we're working with. "He loves movies" is somehow the best-case scenario when two major studios merge.

## The Math

Martin Moszkowicz nailed it: "Fewer global decision-makers ultimately mean fewer buyers." And fewer buyers means tougher negotiations, lower fees, and more centralized greenlight processes. When there are five people who can say yes to your project instead of seven, your leverage drops. When it's three instead of five, you're basically pitching into a void.

Stuart Ford from AGC Studios went further: "If the culture becomes one of everybody's an Uber driver, and we're all just working for the big guy, we're going to lose talent coming into this business, generationally." He's talking about backend participation — the thing that used to make producing a viable career instead of a glorified day rate. Streaming already gutted producer backends. Now consolidation is gutting the number of buyers.

The WGA called the whole thing "a disaster for writers, consumers and the entire entertainment industry."

## The Dead Middle

Here's what nobody at the Senate hearings is saying clearly enough: the $5 million to $60 million movie is already gone. The smart comedy, the adult drama, the non-franchise thriller — extinct at the studio level. Consolidation didn't kill it. Consolidation is just burying the body.

What's left is the $200 million tentpole and the $2 million genre play. Everything in between — the stuff that won Oscars and launched careers — doesn't have a home anymore.

## The Numbers

41,000 film and TV jobs lost in the last three years. Sixteen thousand IATSE members lost their health insurance because they couldn't hit their hours minimums. Paramount's targeting $6 billion in "synergies," and we all know what synergies means. It means people.

If you're a coordinator, a line producer, a production manager — the people I've spent my career working alongside — none of this changes your Tuesday. You're still going to be on set, still drowning in receipts, still trying to close out a job while the industry reshuffles above you. The mergers don't care about your wrap. They never did.

California AG Rob Bonta has an open investigation. A coalition of blue-state attorneys general are looking into it. Whether any of that actually stops the deal is another question.
