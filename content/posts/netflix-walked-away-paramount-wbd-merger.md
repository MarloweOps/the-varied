Netflix had 4 business days to match Paramount's $110 billion offer for Warner Bros. Discovery. They took an hour and ten minutes to say no. Then their stock jumped 13%.

That tells you everything you need to know.

I spent six years in production — commercial, branded content, music video. I've watched studios consolidate, watched buyers disappear, watched the middle of this industry get hollowed out while everyone at the top celebrates "synergies." So when Netflix walked away from the biggest media acquisition in history, I felt something I haven't felt about a corporate decision in a long time: relief.

## The Deal Nobody Should Want

Paramount Skydance is buying Warner Bros. Discovery for $31 a share. All cash. $110 billion in enterprise value. When it closes — probably Q3 this year — David Ellison will control Paramount Pictures, Warner Bros., HBO, CNN, CBS, Discovery, and roughly 15,000 films in the combined library.

That's not a media company. That's a country.

Netflix originally bid $82.7 billion for the streaming and studio assets — not the cable channels, because Netflix doesn't want cable, because nobody wants cable. When Paramount came in higher, Netflix had the option to match. Ted Sarandos called it "a nice-to-have at the right price, not a must-have at any price." Then they collected their $2.8 billion breakup fee and went home.

That $2.8 billion is going straight into a $20 billion content budget for 2026. Instead of buying someone else's library, they're funding original work. I cannot stress enough how rare it is for a company this size to choose building over buying.

## What Producers Are Actually Saying

The producer reaction has been... resigned. Jerry Bruckheimer, at the PGA Awards, said the quiet part out loud: "It's sad. A lot of people will lose their jobs unfortunately." Then he added that David Ellison loves movies, so at least there's that.

That's the level of optimism we're working with. "He loves movies" is somehow the best-case scenario when two major studios merge.

Martin Moszkowicz — the guy behind the Resident Evil franchise — nailed it: "Fewer global decision-makers ultimately mean fewer buyers." And fewer buyers means tougher negotiations, lower fees, and more centralized greenlight processes. The math isn't complicated. When there are five people who can say yes to your project instead of seven, your leverage drops. When it's three instead of five, you're basically pitching into a void.

Stuart Ford from AGC Studios went further: "If the culture becomes one of everybody's an Uber driver, and we're all just working for the big guy, we're going to lose talent coming into this business, generationally." He's talking about backend participation — the thing that used to make producing a viable career instead of a glorified day rate. Streaming already gutted producer backends. Now consolidation is gutting the number of buyers. Pick your poison.

The WGA called the whole thing "a disaster for writers, consumers and the entire entertainment industry." Then they added that regardless of whether Netflix or Paramount won the bid, "whoever wins, writers lose." Hard to argue with that.

## The Middle Is Already Dead

Here's the thing nobody at these Senate hearings is saying clearly enough: the $5 million to $60 million movie is already gone. The smart comedy, the adult drama, the non-franchise thriller — extinct at the studio level. Consolidation didn't kill it. Consolidation is just burying the body.

What's left is the $200 million tentpole and the $2 million genre play. Everything in between — the stuff that used to be the backbone of this industry, the stuff that won Oscars and launched careers — doesn't have a home anymore. Not at the studios. Not on the platforms. Maybe at A24 if you're lucky and your movie stars someone from a Marvel franchise who wants to do "real work" for a few months.

The merged Paramount-WBD entity will carry somewhere between $78 and $100 billion in debt. When you're that leveraged, you don't take creative risks. You make sequels, you mine IP, you greenlight things that look like other things that already worked. That's not a content strategy. That's a spreadsheet strategy.

## The CNN Thing

I don't usually wade into media politics, but it's impossible to talk about this deal without mentioning that David Ellison spent $150 million to acquire The Free Press, installed Bari Weiss as editor-in-chief of CBS News, and is now about to own CNN too.

A veteran CBS News producer — 40 years at the network — just resigned because of what she called "instructions to aim our reporting at a particular part of the political spectrum." A "60 Minutes" segment on deportations got spiked after Trump officials refused to be interviewed. Sharyn Alfonsi, the correspondent, said publicly: "Not an editorial decision. A political one."

I make content for a living. The idea that the person who controls the studio also controls the news is not a production industry problem — it's an everyone problem. But it's worth noting that the same consolidation forces squeezing producers out of backend deals are also concentrating media ownership in ways that would've been unthinkable ten years ago.

## Why Netflix Walking Away Matters

Netflix collecting $2.8 billion to not buy a company is the most Netflix thing Netflix has ever done. But it's more than just smart finance.

When the biggest streaming company in the world says "we'd rather build than buy," it signals something. It says there's still a path that doesn't involve consolidation. It says original content — made by real people, not inherited from a library — still has value. It says you can compete without owning everything.

Maybe I'm reading too much into a corporate M&A decision. But as someone who's been on set at 5am wondering if this industry has a future for people like me, it felt like the first good news in a while.

Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both upgraded Netflix to "Strong Buy" after the withdrawal. The analysts called it "exceptional capital discipline." I'd call it something simpler: knowing when to walk away.

## What This Actually Means For People Who Make Things

If you're an independent producer, the math just got worse. One fewer buyer. Tougher negotiations. More centralized greenlights. The WGA is right — this merger should face serious antitrust scrutiny. California AG Rob Bonta has an open investigation. A coalition of blue-state attorneys general are looking into it. Whether any of that actually stops the deal is another question.

If you're crew — 41,000 film and TV jobs have been lost in the last three years. Sixteen thousand IATSE members lost their health insurance because they couldn't hit their hours minimums. Paramount's targeting $6 billion in "synergies," and we all know what synergies means. It means people.

If you're a coordinator, a line producer, a production manager — the people I've spent my career working alongside — none of this changes your Tuesday. You're still going to be on set, still drowning in receipts, still trying to close out a job while the industry reshuffles above you. The mergers don't care about your wrap. They never did.

But here's what I keep coming back to: Netflix proved you can build instead of buy. You can invest $20 billion in making new things instead of spending $110 billion to own old things. That's not nothing. In an industry obsessed with consolidation, one company chose creation.

I'm glad they walked away. Good for them.
