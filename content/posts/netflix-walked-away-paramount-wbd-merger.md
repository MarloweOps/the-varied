Netflix had 4 business days to match Paramount's $110 billion offer for Warner Bros. Discovery. They took an hour and ten minutes to say no. Then their stock jumped 13%.

That tells you everything you need to know.

I spent six years in production — commercial, branded content, music video. I've watched studios consolidate, watched buyers disappear, watched the middle of this industry get hollowed out while everyone at the top celebrates "synergies." So when Netflix walked away from the biggest media acquisition in history, I felt something I haven't felt about a corporate decision in a long time: relief.

## The Deal

Paramount Skydance is buying Warner Bros. Discovery for $31 a share. All cash. $110 billion in enterprise value. When it closes — probably Q3 — David Ellison will control Paramount Pictures, Warner Bros., HBO, CNN, CBS, Discovery, and roughly 15,000 films.

That's not a media company. That's a country.

Netflix originally bid $82.7 billion for the streaming and studio assets — not the cable channels, because nobody wants cable. Ted Sarandos called it "a nice-to-have at the right price, not a must-have at any price." Then they collected their $2.8 billion breakup fee and went home.

That $2.8 billion is going straight into a $20 billion content budget for 2026. Instead of buying someone else's library, they're funding original work.

## The Stock Market Loved It

Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both upgraded Netflix to "Strong Buy" after the withdrawal. The analysts called it "exceptional capital discipline." Wall Street rewarding a company for *not* spending money is rare. Wall Street rewarding a media company for choosing to build instead of acquire is almost unheard of.

The signal is clear: the market believes Netflix's original content engine is worth more than any library they could buy. $20 billion in new production investment vs. $110 billion in legacy debt and cable infrastructure. The math isn't close.

## Build Over Buy

When the biggest streaming company in the world says "we'd rather build than buy," it signals something. Original content — made by real people, not inherited from a library — still has value. You can compete without owning everything.

Meanwhile the merged Paramount-WBD entity will carry somewhere between $78 and $100 billion in debt. When you're that leveraged, you don't take creative risks. You make sequels, you mine IP, you greenlight things that look like other things that already worked. That's not a content strategy. That's a spreadsheet strategy.

Maybe I'm reading too much into a corporate M&A decision. But as someone who's been on set at 5am wondering if this industry has a future for people like me, it felt like the first good news in a while.

I'm glad they walked away. Good for them.
